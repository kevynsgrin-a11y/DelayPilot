The rules that apply to you depend on where you flew from, where you were going, who operated the aircraft, and what the airline says caused the problem. You will not know all of that standing at a desk, and you do not need to. What you need at the desk is an order of operations that does not destroy options you might want later, because the frameworks share one structural feature: several of them frame a cancellation as a choice you make, and a choice you have already made is difficult to unmake.

## What determines what you can ask for

Four facts do most of the work, and three of them are fixed the moment the cancellation happens.

**Where the journey touched.** Departure point, arrival point, and the operating carrier decide which framework reaches your flight. This is not the airline whose name is on your ticket if another carrier actually flew the aircraft.

**How your flights were sold.** One reservation covering the whole journey behaves very differently from two tickets you bought separately. If you connected yourself, the second flight is generally a separate journey, and the airline operating it did not lose you — from its side, you did not arrive.

**When you were told.** Notice is a live test in more than one framework. The message that told you, with its timestamp, is evidence. Screenshot it before the app refreshes and replaces it with the new itinerary.

**What the airline says happened, and who says so.** An airline-stated reason is an assertion. It is not a determination by a regulator or a court, and it is not proved by the weather you can see out of the window. It still matters, because it is the input that most often decides whether anything beyond rebooking and a refund is in play.

One thing does not depend on cause at all in several frameworks: the obligation to look after you while you wait. Ask for it even while the cause is being argued about.

## What you still need to know

You will probably leave the airport without knowing the cause classification, and that is normal. What you should not leave without is the ability to reconstruct the day: the original schedule, the message announcing the cancellation, the name and number of the flight you were moved to, the time you actually arrived, and receipts for anything you had to buy. Those five items are what turns "I think I might have a case" into an assessment somebody can actually run.

If you are unsure whether your flights were on one reservation, that question has its own guide, because it changes both the connection analysis and the rights analysis. See [self-transfer risk](/guides/self-transfer-risk/) and [missed connection on one ticket](/guides/missed-connection-on-one-ticket/).

## What to do now, in order

The order below runs from reversible to irreversible. Do the early items even if you are certain about the later ones.

1. **Screenshot everything before it changes.** The original itinerary, the cancellation message with its timestamp, and the departure board if it shows a status. This takes under a minute and cannot be recreated tomorrow.

2. **Get in two queues at once.** Join the physical desk queue and start the airline's app, phone line, or chat at the same time. Whichever answers first is the one you use. This is free and costs you nothing if both work.

3. **Ask what they can put you on — without accepting yet.** "What are my options?" is a different sentence from "yes". Ask about the airline's own later flights, its partners, and other airports in the same city. Write the options down.

4. **Ask for care while you wait.** Food, a way to communicate, and somewhere to sleep if the wait runs overnight. In several frameworks this obligation does not depend on the cause of the disruption. If the airline does not provide it, buy something reasonable and keep the receipt, because reimbursement of a reasonable expense is a different question from compensation.

5. **Decide between travelling and not travelling.** This is the fork. Being rerouted, travelling later, or asking for the fare back are separate choices with separate consequences, and which ones are available to you depends on the framework and the facts. Read the relevant explainer before you commit: [United States](/passenger-rights/us/), [European Union](/passenger-rights/eu/), [United Kingdom](/passenger-rights/uk/), [Canada](/passenger-rights/canada/).

6. **Do not accept a voucher or credit while you are still deciding.** A voucher is the airline's product. Accepting it can end the question of getting your money back. If someone is pressing you to accept quickly, that is a reason to slow down, not to hurry.

7. **Ask for the reason in writing.** One sentence by email: please confirm the reason recorded for the cancellation of this flight. The answer is an assertion, not a finding, and it is still where every framework's cause test starts.

8. **File with the airline first, then escalate.** Every framework expects you to ask the airline before you ask anyone else. If the airline's answer does not match its own published position, the regulator's complaint route is the next step, and it is free.

## Two things that quietly cost people money

**Leaving the airport without asking for care.** People assume that a weather cancellation removes every obligation. In several frameworks the care obligation is not conditional on fault, and a reasonable receipted expense is a bounded, documented ask that does not wait on the cause argument.

**Accepting the first rebooking without asking what else exists.** The first option offered is the one the airline's system surfaced first, not necessarily the one that suits your onward plans. Asking about partner airlines and nearby airports costs one question.

There is a specific moment at which people buy travel insurance: after the storm is named, after the strike is announced, after the airline's app has started rearranging itineraries. It is the most understandable purchase in travel and the one most likely to produce an argument later, because it is exactly the moment the policy's own definitions start doing work.

## The contract is the answer, and nothing else is

A travel policy is a commercial contract between you and an insurer. Its wording defines what is covered, when cover attaches, what evidence it requires, what it excludes, and how long you have to tell them. No article, comparison site, or assistant — including this one — can tell you what your policy does, because the answer is a document that varies by insurer, by product, by country of sale, and by version.

That is not a hedge. It is the single most useful thing to know, because it changes where you look: the definitions section of your own certificate rather than a general explanation of travel insurance.

## Why the purchase moment matters

Insurance prices and covers uncertainty. Once an event is publicly known, it is no longer uncertain in the same way, and policies address that with their own definitions — of when cover attaches, of what counts as a known or announced event at the time of purchase, and of which benefits are affected.

Where the line sits for your policy is written in your policy. What is general is only this: the question will be asked, the date and time of purchase is a fact the insurer holds, and the answer will not be improved by hoping nobody checks.

If you are reading this while a disruption is already underway, the honest expectation is that a policy bought now is being bought for the rest of the trip and for things that have not happened yet, and that the insurer will assess the current event against its own definitions.

## Insurance does not replace the statutory layers

This is where the two subjects meet, and where people lose money by assuming one substitutes for the other.

The passenger-rights frameworks operate on the airline, not on your insurer. In the US, the federal refund layer attaches to defined events and to your decision to decline what the airline offered. In the EU, the framework can require care while you wait and a choice between rerouting and reimbursement after a cancellation, subject to its own conditions. Those obligations exist whether or not you hold a policy, and an insurer generally expects you to pursue what is available from the airline first — which is another reason to keep the documentation either way.

Being insured also does not make an airline's obligation go away, and being uninsured does not remove one. Treat them as two separate files, with two separate correspondents, and do not let a claim handler's timetable stop you from asking the airline in time.

## What to read in your policy, in this order

1. **The definitions.** Every contentious word — delay, cancellation, known event, trip interruption, reasonable expense — is defined, and the definitions are usually not what the ordinary meaning would suggest.
2. **When cover attached.** Purchase date, effective date, and any waiting period are separate ideas.
3. **What triggers each benefit,** and the evidence required for each. Some benefits require documentation from the airline that is easier to get on the day than a month later.
4. **The exclusions.** Read these before the benefits. They are shorter and they decide more.
5. **The notification requirement.** Most policies require you to tell the insurer within a defined period, and a late notification is a self-inflicted problem.
6. **The claims process itself,** including how the insurer wants to be contacted at an inconvenient hour.

## What you still need to know

Three things, and none requires an insurer to answer: when you bought the policy, what your certificate says under definitions and exclusions, and what the airline has offered you so far. The third often reduces the insurance question considerably — a refund or a rebooking from the airline changes what is left to claim.

## What to do now, in order

1. **Find the actual certificate,** not the marketing page you bought from. The wording is the thing.
2. **Note your purchase date and time** against the date the event became public. You will be asked.
3. **Notify the insurer early if you may claim.** Notification is not the same as claiming and is usually required sooner than people expect.
4. **Pursue the airline in parallel,** because those obligations are separate and have their own timetables.
5. **Keep receipts and the airline's written statements** in one place. Both files draw on the same evidence.
6. **Do not buy a new policy expecting it to cover today's event** without first reading its definitions of when cover attaches.
